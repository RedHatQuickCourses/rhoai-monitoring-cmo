Observability Foundations

Introduction

Imagine you are an AI platform engineer. Your team deployed a large language model on OpenShift AI to power an internal chatbot. On Monday morning, users start complaining: "The chatbot is really slow today." Your manager asks: "Is it the model? The GPU? The network? Are we getting more traffic than usual?"

You open the OpenShift console and see the pod is Running, CPU usage is 12%, memory looks fine. Everything looks green. But users are still waiting 30 seconds for a response.

The answer is that traditional Kubernetes metrics cannot tell you what is happening inside an AI inference workload. The pod is running, but the GPU’s KV cache is full, requests are queuing, and the model is bottlenecked on GPU memory. You would never know this from cpu_usage and memory_working_set.

This is why AI model serving requires a different observability strategy — and that is what this course is about.

What You Will Learn

This chapter establishes the foundational concepts that every subsequent chapter builds on:

  • AI vs Traditional Monitoring — Why AI model serving workloads need different monitoring than traditional web applications

  • Three Layers of Observability — A mental model for understanding the three layers of metrics you need for AI serving

  • OpenShift Monitoring Stack — How Prometheus, Thanos Querier, ServiceMonitors, and DCGM Exporter work together

  • Enable User Workload Monitoring — Hands-on lab to enable UWM and verify metrics are flowing

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Explain why container-level metrics (CPU, memory) are insufficient for AI model serving workloads

  2. Describe the three layers of AI serving observability and what each layer reveals

  3. Explain the role of Prometheus, Thanos Querier, ServiceMonitors, and DCGM Exporter in the OpenShift monitoring stack

  4. Enable User Workload Monitoring and verify that GPU metrics are queryable from the OpenShift Console
